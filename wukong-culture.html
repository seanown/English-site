When a Videogame Becomes Cultural Diplomacy

Why a videogame exhibition, a city-wide animation festival and a West Lake night cruise may matter more to Macao than any contract signed on the trip.

Sean Own   ·   May 2025   ·   China

The cultural stop was supposed to be a rest day. It turned into the most important one. We started at the China Academy of Art, where the 'Black Myth: Wukong' art exhibition was drawing queues three deep into the early afternoon. The exhibition is the first time a Chinese AAA videogame has been treated as fine art — scale renderings, silk-printed backstory panels, life-size character studies, and a quiet gallery upstairs with production paintings.

What struck us was the demographic. The room was full of people in their twenties and thirties, paying full price to look at a game they had played. The cultural implication is obvious: the Chinese cultural export pipeline is not opera, not calligraphy — it is the thing your daughter spent eighty hours with last winter. Macau's role is to be the licensing hall for that pipeline.

The afternoon took us across town to the Hangzhou International Animation Festival, where provincial and independent studios were showing IP for licensing across Asia. Three of the booths were pitching Macau as a permanent licensing venue — before we had said a word. The market signal is unmistakable.

We ended on West Lake. The night-cruise version of the show, with the LED 'Memories of Hangzhou' signature lit up in the harbour, was built by the same Zhang Yimou creative team that opened the 2008 Olympics. It runs every night, on both banks, free. For a Macao delegation it was a sharp reminder: you don't outspend a city on culture, you out-build one. The next stop is back home.

You don't outspend a city on culture. You out-build one.
