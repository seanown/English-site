Insights · Reading Notes · Kishore Mahbubani

Mahbubani in 49 Questions

Understanding the China–US rivalry of the century: strategy, China's system, Taiwan, ASEAN and a multipolar world, through one Singaporean diplomat's arguments.

Sean Own   ·   November 2026   ·   Macao

The answers below paraphrase arguments Kishore Mahbubani has made in his books and interviews. They are summaries, not direct quotations.

I. The Structure of the China–US Contest (Questions 1–10)

Q01: What is the root cause of the geopolitical conflict between China and the United States?

It is the reflex history has shown for two thousand years: the established number-one power moves to stop a rising rival from overtaking it. That is an iron law of geopolitics. Even if China became a full Western-style democracy tomorrow, the United States would still try just as hard to contain its growth.

Q02: What is America's biggest strategic mistake in confronting China?

The United States launched this contest without first defining a clear, long-term end goal. Washington cannot stop China's economic rise, cannot overthrow the Communist Party, and cannot isolate China completely, so it is locked in a confrontation with no stated objective.

Q03: What is the biggest strategic mistake China has made in dealing with the United States?

Through unequal treatment of foreign companies and forced technology transfer, China alienated the American business community, once its most reliable ally in Washington. Combined with some hard-line rhetoric after the 2008 financial crisis, this meant that when Trump launched the trade war in 2018, American business no longer lobbied on China's behalf.

Q04: Can the trade war and technology tariffs bring down China's economy?

Tariffs and sanctions have not held China back. They have instead hurt American companies, workers and consumers. China has become a bigger retail and manufacturing market than the United States, and the semiconductor embargo has eroded the profits that American chipmakers rely on to fund research.

Q05: How do the two countries' strategic cultures differ?

America plays chess, aiming for a quick checkmate. China plays Go (weiqi), which rewards long-term patience and positioning, shifting the strategic balance slowly by accumulating territory at the edges.

Q06: Is the United States wrongly applying its Cold War playbook against the Soviet Union to China?

Yes. It is the classic error of fighting tomorrow's war with yesterday's strategy. China is deeply woven into global trade and has no interest in exporting its ideology. It bears no resemblance to the isolated, closed Soviet Union.

Q07: Is Washington's anxiety about China's rise partly irrational?

Deep in the Western psyche lies a historical, largely unconscious fear of the "Yellow Peril" and of a non-white civilisation becoming a great power. That emotional reaction undermines Washington's ability to craft a rational response, and breaks Sun Tzu's rule of knowing both yourself and your opponent.

Q08: Has the world accepted America's framing of the contest as democracy versus authoritarianism?

No. Large developing democracies such as India and Indonesia do not feel threatened by China's system. Most countries care more about their own development and mutually beneficial trade, and they reject this binary ideological mobilisation.

Q09: Can China and the United States still achieve a win-win outcome in this century?

They must. Facing existential threats such as climate change and pandemics, the two countries have no choice but to cooperate. Globalisation has put them on the same ship, and either side trying to sink the other's cabin will only sink everyone.

Q10: Measured by George Kennan's four criteria, who is ahead?

Kennan stressed a clear goal, solving problems at home, carrying global responsibilities, and spiritual vitality. On all four, China currently shows more momentum. The United States, by contrast, is weakening its own long-term competitiveness through domestic political division and unilateral withdrawals from international commitments.

II. China's System, Governance and Civilisation (Questions 11–20)

Q11: What kind of system does China actually run today?

Modern China does not run on doctrinaire communism. It runs a new form of state capitalism, one that has absorbed the best practices of modern capitalism and free markets and made China a formidable market competitor.

Q12: If China runs a market economy, why does its ruling party still call itself "Communist"?

The name comes from the party's founding history. In substance, it has become a "Chinese Civilisation Party," dedicated to the revival of a multi-ethnic civilisation. Its soul lies in the survival and prosperity of four thousand years of Chinese civilisation, not in promoting Marxism-Leninism.

Q13: Why was the hope that an opened-up China would become an American-style liberal democracy bound to fail?

Expecting a four-thousand-year-old civilisation to copy the system of a country less than 250 years old was always arrogant and unrealistic. Modernisation is not Westernisation. China's political identity is rooted in social order and traditional family ethics.

Q14: What core belief in traditional Chinese political culture supports a strong central government?

Chinese tradition holds that a strong, unified central government is what prevents the country from falling into fragmentation and turmoil, delivering "freedom from chaos." People value social harmony, stability and material well-being more than individual rights or opposition to the state.

Q15: How do Chinese people really view the Communist Party's legitimacy?

The party ended the "century of humiliation" and delivered prosperity. Long-running Harvard surveys show satisfaction with the central government above 90 per cent. Over the past forty years, the Chinese people have seen the fastest rise in living standards in their four-thousand-year history.

Q16: How does Mahbubani compare the two countries' political systems?

Under the influence of money, the United States has drifted into a plutocracy that serves the top 1 per cent. China, by contrast, practises a meritocracy that selects and promotes officials on the basis of evaluation and ability.

Q17: Will a stronger China invade other countries the way Western empires did?

History suggests not. Chinese civilisation carries a strong non-expansionist DNA; for most of two thousand years it was an agrarian power on the defensive. Even in the fifteenth century, when Zheng He commanded the world's most powerful ocean-going fleet, China founded no colonies anywhere.

Q18: How do leaders outside the West see Xi Jinping?

Most international leaders who have dealt with Xi Jinping describe him as clear-headed, rational and pragmatic. Across Asia, he is seen as a leader determined to end the century of humiliation and revive Chinese civilisation, not as an ideologue exporting revolution.

Q19: How do non-Western countries respond to Western moral criticism of China on human rights and authoritarianism?

Most countries hold to the UN Charter principle of non-interference in internal affairs and focus pragmatically on building mutually beneficial economic ties with China. The international community sees the West's double standards on human rights and understands that geopolitics is ultimately about power, not morality.

Q20: Why have Western predictions of a "coming collapse of China" been wrong for decades?

Western analysts underestimated the drive of 1.4 billion people seeking prosperity, and the government's ability to correct problems step by step. Since the West first predicted collapse in 1990, China's economy has grown many times over.

III. Taiwan and the Strait (Questions 21–28)

Q21: Where does Taiwan sit in China–US relations?

Taiwan is the most dangerous red line in the relationship, the one flashpoint that could trigger a direct war between nuclear powers. For Beijing, Taiwan is the last unhealed wound of the century of humiliation, and moving towards reunification is a historical mission it cannot give up.

Q22: What role does Taiwan risk playing on the China–US board?

Taiwan risks being sacrificed as a pawn in the larger game. A chess player will give up a pawn without hesitation to win the whole board, and visits to Taiwan staged by Washington politicians often bring Taiwan real danger.

Q23: Is pursuing "de jure independence" a realistic path for Taiwan's survival and development?

If Taiwan gave up the Republic of China passport for a "Republic of Taiwan" one, it would face total international isolation and catastrophic risk. Almost no country in the world would recognise an independent Taiwan. Maintaining the status quo is the most rational line of defence for its survival.

Q24: What is the best strategy for Taiwan's people to keep their current way of life?

The most pragmatic route is to accept, at least nominally, a place within a "one China" framework while securing a high degree of internal autonomy and openness to the world. Since the international community does not recognise Taiwan as a sovereign state, seeking peaceful coexistence is far safer than a rush to independence.

Q25: What political cornerstone has kept the peace across the Strait for decades?

The key is maintaining the status quo, including the "1992 Consensus," a tacit understanding that has helped keep the peace across the Strait. That understanding contains a measure of fiction, but if it were broken, it would immediately push both sides to the brink of military conflict.

Q26: Will Beijing rush to use force against Taiwan in the near term?

On Taiwan, Beijing is playing a long game of Go, confident that time and the balance of overall strength are on its side. China seeks to win without fighting. Unless Taiwan actually crosses the red line of de jure independence, it will not lightly start a war.

Q27: What Asian political philosophy is reflected in direct dialogue between leaders on both sides of the Strait?

That leaders on the two sides can hold high-level talks despite deep political differences reflects an Asian pragmatism the West finds hard to understand. Asian cultures are used to coexisting with grey areas and contradictions, and avoid the West's black-and-white, zero-sum ruptures.

Q28: Is it reasonable to compare the Taiwan Strait with the Ukraine crisis?

No. Ukraine is a sovereign state with a seat at the United Nations, while the great majority of countries regard Taiwan as part of China under international law. Treating the two as the same ignores historical and legal realities and only feeds more emotional, irrational decision-making.

IV. Neighbours, Regional Diplomacy and ASEAN Wisdom (Questions 29–37)

Q29: Whose dispute is the South China Sea, really?

At heart, it is a local dispute between China and four ASEAN members: Vietnam, Malaysia, Brunei and the Philippines. It is not a direct China–US confrontation, and the parties involved have long exercised strategic restraint to avoid war.

Q30: What strategic mistake has the Philippines made in handling its South China Sea dispute with China?

The Philippines swings sharply between pro-American and pro-Chinese positions roughly every five years as governments change, showing little strategic steadiness next to a great power. A small country should build a consistent long-term foreign policy and avoid becoming the front-line proxy of a single outside power.

Q31: What can Southeast Asia learn from Vietnam's thousand years of living next to China?

Vietnam shows the balance a leader needs: the courage to stand firm on principle and push back, together with the ability to live in harmony with China. Actively repairing political trust after each dispute is how a small country secures lasting peace beside a giant neighbour.

Q32: Should Asia copy Europe's NATO model, or revive the Southeast Asia Treaty Organization (SEATO)?

No. NATO works by defining an external enemy and organising exclusive confrontation, which makes it a failed model for Asia. ASEAN's success comes precisely from including every major power and treating none as an enemy.

Q33: The ten Southeast Asian countries differ widely in systems and culture. How have they kept peace and prosperity for more than forty years?

ASEAN relies on the pragmatism of "consultation, consensus and non-interference," and that has produced a peace miracle. ASEAN does not play the West's black-and-white, zero-sum game; it is skilled at holding the whole together amid ambiguity.

Q34: How should we read Australia's diplomatic strategy in the China–US contest?

Australian thinking has long been captured by Washington, and the country lacks the self-defined role a middle power in Asia should have. It lectures China from a position of assumed superiority, yet by 2045 the leading power in the Asia-Pacific is far more likely to be Beijing than Washington.

Q35: How should smaller neighbours respond to a resurgent China?

Living next to an elephant requires flexible strategic adjustment: never kowtowing, but never provoking either. There is no such thing as a benevolent great power in geopolitics, so small and medium states must rely on diplomatic skill to secure room for autonomy.

Q36: What has the Belt and Road Initiative actually done for Southeast Asia?

Through railways, ports and other infrastructure, China has tied Southeast Asia's economic interests closely to its own. ASEAN has now overtaken the US and the EU as China's largest trading partner, and the two sides are highly interdependent.

Q37: Does the Western charge of Chinese "debt-trap diplomacy" in developing countries hold up?

Countries need real capital to build infrastructure, and Chinese financing is far more tangible than the West's empty promises. Most countries in the Global South chose to work with China on their own terms; they did not passively fall into a so-called trap.

V. A Multipolar World, Historical Normality and the Future of Governance (Questions 38–49)

Q38: What is the basis for the "return of the Asian century"?

From the year 1 to 1820, the world's two largest economies were consistently China and India. The West's dominance over the past two hundred years is a historical aberration; Asia's return to the centre of the world economy is simply history returning to normal.

Q39: What does the metaphor of "boats becoming cabins" mean?

Humanity used to live in 193 separate boats. Today we live in 193 cabins on the same ship. Against transnational crises such as climate change and pandemics, looking after only your own cabin, or attacking someone else's, will sink everyone.

Q40: What kind of "strategic brain damage" did the Western strategic community suffer after the Cold War?

Francis Fukuyama's "end of history" thesis led the West to believe liberal democracy was the final stage of human development, and it fell into deep arrogance. That blind confidence made the West overlook the resilience of non-Western civilisations and left it unable to adapt to a multipolar world.

Q41: What is the "3M strategy" Mahbubani recommends for the West in the twenty-first century?

The West should become minimalist, multilateralist and Machiavellian: cut back on pointless military intervention abroad, use multilateral rules to constrain future great powers, and focus on its own core interests.

Q42: What long-term risks come from America weaponising the dollar through unilateral sanctions?

Turning the dollar, a global public good, into a weapon will speed up de-dollarisation and local-currency settlement by China, Russia and the Global South. Over time, this undermines the Achilles' heel of American economic hegemony and risks backfiring on its own financial position.

Q43: Is China building institutions such as the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB) to overturn the international order?

China has benefited greatly from the existing global system. Its new institutions make up for the under-representation of emerging economies in current financial governance. While reform of the Western-led World Bank has stalled, the AIIB shows a more efficient and inclusive model of cross-border governance.

Q44: Will China replace the United States in promoting its ideology and way of life around the world?

China does not believe it has a universal mission to spread the Chinese way of life to all of humanity. It respects the distinctiveness of other civilisations and cultures, and its foreign policy focuses on protecting the livelihoods and stability of 1.4 billion people.

Q45: How do African countries really respond to Western criticism of China's growing influence in Africa?

African countries deeply resent the West's long habit of lecturing them from above, and value their right to decide for themselves how to deal with both the United States and China. China has built roads, railways and power grids that Africa badly needs, and has genuinely helped local economies grow.

Q46: Will the world's major middle powers be forced to choose between the United States and China?

Most countries need the United States for security and China for its vast market, and they refuse to pick a side. Flexible hedging and diversified cooperation have become the common way countries maximise their strategic autonomy.

Q47: With geopolitical conflict intensifying, are the United Nations and other multilateral institutions obsolete?

The United Nations remains humanity's indispensable global village council, and the only platform that gives international cooperation legal legitimacy. The Security Council urgently needs reform to include emerging powers such as India, but multilateralism remains the cornerstone for preventing major war.

Q48: How does Mahbubani answer critics who call his arguments pro-China?

He sees himself as a warning bell, trying to break the Anglo-Saxon media's monopoly on the conversation. Only by presenting Asian perspectives honestly can the West shed its arrogance and avoid a catastrophic direct clash with China.

Q49: What will ultimately decide the contest between the two powers?

Not military force or ideological slogans, but which side does a better job of solving its own domestic problems of livelihood and governance. Whoever improves its people's well-being, keeps society cohesive and holds its strategic nerve will win this century-long race.
