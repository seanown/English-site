Essay · Macao's Third Five-Year Plan

From One Industry to Many

Why Macao's third Five-Year Plan won't be won in the numbers — but in three Macao-sized questions that nobody has put on paper yet.

Sean Own   ·   3 September 2026   ·   Macao

Chinese do things in five-year arcs. The first Five-Year Plan dates to 1953. Seven decades and fourteen plans later, most of the rest of the world has burned through dozens of ruling parties and twice as many policy lines. We just finished one plan, then started the next, then the next, and refused to spend the in-between years arguing about whether to start. Macao is twenty-seven years past the handover. It is now walking into its own third Five-Year Plan. That, on its own, is worth pausing on.

The plan published on 18 August 2026. Ten chapters in thirty-six sections. Around 86,000 characters. Thirty-five headline indicators, sixty-eight special projects, three hundred and ten defined workstreams. None of that arrived overnight. Drafting took roughly eighteen months. Forty pre-draft field studies. Thirty-six commissioned research papers. Forty days of public consultation. The final text runs about 30,000 characters longer than the consultation draft, and having lived in Macao for thirteen years — watching the city argue with itself about whether it could ever be more than gaming — I know what those extra thirty thousand characters actually are. They are not paragraphs. They are opinions someone put in, and opinions someone agreed to listen to.

Two numbers from the document stay with me. The first is roughly MOP 130 billion in committed investment across the headline project portfolio. For a micro-economy, that is unprecedented scale. It also tells you the government has stopped being the drafter and started being the operator. The second is the binding target: non-gaming value-added as a share of GDP climbing to about sixty per cent by 2030. That number is not for the press release. It is a constraint. Every annual budget, every industrial policy, every performance review will be tested against it. Putting a stake in the ground is what it looks like.

On the headline direction, I am cautiously optimistic. The four flagship projects each take a different slice. The Macao-Hengqin International Education (University) City stitches the talent layer. The Macao Science & R&D Industrial Park adds the research layer. The Macao International Integrated Tourism & Culture Zone defends the cultural layer. The Hengqin forward cargo terminal at Macau International Airport opens the logistics layer. Read end-to-end, those four form one chain, not a sprinkle of pepper.

But on whether it lands — on that, I am openly sceptical. Twenty-five years in the Greater Bay Area teaches you that beautiful plans die on the paper they were printed on. I have seen cities spend real money on industrial parks, build them, then leave them empty. The buildings were ready, the industry was not, the talent was not, and no one had bothered to give any of them a reason to stay. Money is the easiest part of this. The hard part is making sure the MOP 130 billion leaves behind industries that know how to fund themselves — not pretty buildings and a list of payments due in five years.

These last few years I have sat across from plenty of Macao SMEs. Dozens of souvenir shops that have been open for decades. Their owners do not lack the will to go online — they lack the map. Macao's "Creative City of Gastronomy" tenure is approaching ten years. Half our restaurants cook as well as anywhere in the region. Most of them cannot tell their own story. Too sincere, too literal. You cannot fault them for it. Structural problems do not respond to individual heroics, and they do not get solved by individuals either. They get solved when someone lays down the path and walks the first mile beside them.

So when I read the plan, I am not reading the big words. I am reading where it lands. Will the residents of the forty-year-old walk-up blocks in Iaulan — the Yau Han block (祐漢) most of us drive past on the way to somewhere else — be in refurbished homes by 2030? Will the wet-market stallholder of twenty years pick up a phone, take an order, and ship it across the strait? Will a Macao graduate, walking out of university this June, have a third door — beyond the integrated resort, beyond the civil service? If those three questions get a real answer, then the sixty per cent starts to mean something. If they do not, the share will tick up anyway — and it will only mean money moved from one pocket to another.

The plan says what it should. Comprehensive SME digitalisation. Stepped-up district revitalisation. Getting tourists off the main avenues and into neighbourhood eateries and shops. All of that is correct. But there is one thing the document cannot write into a clause: the operator doing this has to have the patience to sit with one bakery for three years. Digitalisation is not buying a system or opening a storefront. It is taking thirty years of craft, translating it into words a stranger can hear, and shipping it to someone willing to pay for it. It will not make the news. It will not get a photo. But Macao's diversified economy, in the end, is going to be propped up one such small shop at a time.

This city has done something for four hundred years that almost nowhere else has: let East and West live side by side on the same street without asking either to convert the other. That composure is Macao's most precious asset. It is also the spine of the central government's one centre, one platform, one base positioning for the city. The next five years are not about Macao turning into another Shenzhen or another Singapore. They are about that composure being turned, finally, into a product, a service, something someone is willing to cross the water for.

Money is the easiest part. The hard part is making sure MOP 130 billion leaves behind industries that know how to fund themselves.

"One part planning, nine parts execution," the Chief Executive said at the press briefing after the plan was released. It is worth pinning that to the wall, because it matters more than any number in the document. Five years from now we will come back to these 86,000 characters and ask a single, blunt question: how many of them became orders — and how many of them became the quiet composure of a Macao kitchen table.
